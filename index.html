Sources of Drugs (Origins of Medicines)
Animal and human proteins Minerals Elemental metals Plants Synthetic chemicals
Examples: Insulin (animal/human proteins), digitalis (plant — foxglove), iron (elemental metal), aspirin (originally derived from willow bark), morphine (plant — opium poppy), epinephrine (synthetic copy of natural hormone)

AHFS (American Hospital Formulary Services) — evidence-based drug information widely used by clinicians in hospital settings
Drug handbooks — compact clinical references for bedside use
Online resources — real-time drug information; must verify credibility of source
Pharmacology textbooks — foundational drug principles and mechanisms
Drug Regulation
FDA — Food and Drug Administration
Strictly regulates all devices and substances used on or in the body: prescription drugs, OTC drugs, generic drugs, food supplements, cosmetics, medical devices, sutures and wound-closure devices, biologicals
State practice acts
Each state's practice acts govern: procurement and secure storage, prescription/ordering/transcription, preparation and dispensing, and administration of drugs. Institutional policy must conform to state law.
Drug types by access:
Prescription drugs
Require authorization (order) from a licensed prescriber; dispensed by pharmacist; only available to identified patient for specific indication
OTC (over-the-counter)
Available without a prescription; FDA-approved for self-administration; still have risks, interactions, and contraindications
Herbal remedies and food supplements
Not classified as drugs by FDA; less regulated; BUT can have significant interactions with anesthetic and surgical medications — must be disclosed pre-op
Controlled substances (DEA Schedules I–V):
Carry high risk of abuse or addiction; regulated by DEA (Drug Enforcement Administration) in addition to FDA.
Schedule I — highest abuse potential, no accepted medical use (heroin, LSD)
Schedule II — high potential, accepted use with severe restrictions (morphine, fentanyl, oxycodone, cocaine)
Schedule III–V — decreasing abuse potential (codeine combinations, benzodiazepines, cough preparations)
In the OR: Controlled substances (especially Schedule II opioids and anesthetic agents) are strictly logged, counted, and accounted for. Discrepancies must be reported immediately. Wasted drug must be witnessed and documented.
Drug Nomenclature (Naming) & Labeling
Chemical name
The precise chemical composition/structure of the drug; complex and not used clinically (e.g., N-acetyl-para-aminophenol for acetaminophen). Used by chemists and researchers.
Generic name
Official nonproprietary name assigned by USAN (US Adopted Names Council); same drug regardless of manufacturer; not capitalized; e.g., epinephrine, lidocaine, heparin. Used in prescribing and in this course.
Trade name (brand name)
Proprietary name given by the manufacturer; capitalized and registered trademark; a single drug may have multiple trade names. E.g., Adrenalin® (epinephrine), Xylocaine® (lidocaine).
Required elements of a drug label:
Name (generic + trade) Dosage form Amount in package Indications Dosage Route of administration Bar code Lot number Expiration date
Drug formats (dosage forms): Tablet, liquid, cream, powder, suspension, gel, suppository, patch. The format is specifically chosen to be most compatible with how the drug is administered and how it reaches the target tissue. Quick CNS action = liquid IV injection. The ST must correctly prepare different drug formats on the sterile field.
Pregnancy categories (A, B, C, D, X): Indicate potential risk to the fetus. Category X = contraindicated in pregnancy (proven fetal risk). Important for pre-op patient assessment and informed consent in reproductive-age patients.
The 7 Rights of the Medication Process
The 7 Rights are the foundational safety framework for all medication administration. The ST is responsible for applying the 7 Rights for every drug on the sterile field, every time, without exception.
1
Right Drug
Verify the actual drug name against the order or preference card. Check for look-alike, sound-alike drugs. Read the label three times: when selecting, when preparing, when administering/dispensing to the field.
2
Right Patient
Confirm patient identity using two identifiers (name + DOB, name + MRN) before any drug is administered. On the sterile field, the patient is identified via the surgical time-out.
3
Right Dose
Confirm the dose matches the order. For diluted or prepared drugs, calculate carefully. Know maximum doses for local anesthetics (toxicity risk). Verify with circulator using closed-loop communication.
4
Right Route
Confirm the correct administration route (IV, topical, local injection, irrigation, etc.). Route affects drug onset, duration, and concentration. A drug given by wrong route can be immediately lethal (e.g., intrathecal vincristine).
5
Right Time
Administer or prepare at the correct time relative to the surgical procedure. Timing of antibiotics (within 60 min before incision), hemostatics, and vasoconstrictors is clinically critical.
6
Right Indication
Confirm the drug is appropriate for the patient's condition and there are no contraindications (allergies, comorbidities, drug interactions). Verify allergy status before any drug on the field.
7
Right Documentation (Label)
Every medication container on the sterile field must be labeled immediately and completely (name, strength, date/time received). Documentation after administration must include drug name, dose, amount, route, time, and name of person administering.
Elements of a Drug Order / Prescription
A valid prescription must contain all of these elements:
1. Name of the patient
2. Prescriber's name (and credentials/DEA number for controlled substances)
3. Date and time of prescription
4. Name and strength of the drug
5. Dose (amount per administration)
6. Route of administration
7. Time or frequency of administration (how often)
Types of Drug Orders
Verbal order
Order given verbally by prescriber; must be confirmed using closed-loop communication ("read-back"); documented and signed within required timeframe; high risk for error
Written order
Standard written or electronic order in the medical record; most reliable; reduces error potential; basis for medication administration
Standing order
Pre-approved order set for specific clinical situations (e.g., pre-op antibiotic protocol); applies to a class of patients without individual prescriber order each time
Stat order
Immediate administration required ("stat" = statim, Latin for immediately); highest priority; life-threatening or urgent situations
PRN order
"Pro re nata" = as needed; administered when patient meets specific criteria (e.g., pain score ≥ 7, fever > 38.5°C); not on a fixed schedule
Protocol for Dispensing Drugs to the Sterile Field
This is one of the most critically tested topics for the ST. Every step must be followed in sequence. Both the circulator AND the STSR are responsible for verification — no one person acts alone.
Step-by-step sterile field drug protocol:
1
Select appropriate container
Circulator selects appropriate size and type of container for the drug (medicine cup, basin, syringe). Must be compatible with the drug and route.
2
Circulator holds drug for STSR to read
Circulator holds the drug vial/package so that the STSR can clearly read the label from the sterile field — label facing the scrub. Do not toss or hand across the sterile field.
3
Both verify integrity
Both the STSR and circulator check: package seal intact, no cracks, not expired, correct appearance. Do not use if any doubt about integrity.
4
STSR reads label aloud
The STSR reads the drug name, dose/strength, amount, and expiration date out loud using closed-loop communication — this is not optional.
5
Circulator verifies verbally AND visually
Circulator confirms by reading the label themselves and verbally confirming each element. Both parties must independently verify — not just one reading and the other listening.
6
Circulator dispenses drug to the sterile field
Circulator dispenses using aseptic technique: drops into a sterile medicine cup, pours into a basin, or transfers via vial top withdrawal. Does NOT reach over or contaminate sterile field.
7
Label is reconfirmed
After dispensing, the STSR reconfirms what was received. Circulator retains the original vial/package on the non-sterile side for reference throughout the case.
8
Maximum dose is verified
Especially important for local anesthetics — the STSR must know the maximum safe dose for weight-based drugs and track total amount given throughout the case.
9
ALL containers are labeled — immediately
Every medication container on the sterile field is labeled IMMEDIATELY after receiving, before receiving the next drug. Label must include: drug name, strength, and date/time received. Waterproof ink on adhesive label. Legible. "When in doubt, throw it out."
Dispensing to the Surgeon
When handing a drug to the surgeon for administration:
✦ State the drug name and concentration clearly before handing
✦ Confirm verbally that the surgeon received what was asked for
✦ Track the total amount administered from the field
✦ Document on the intraoperative record
Sterile field labeling standards:
✦ Waterproof ink on adhesive label or sticker
✦ Legible — block letters; not abbreviations for drug names
✦ "When in doubt, throw it out" — if a container is unlabeled or label is illegible, discard it
✦ Identify all drugs with personnel changes — when the ST is relieved, the incoming scrub must be informed of all drugs on the field; verbal and visual verification required during handoff
Look-Alike / Sound-Alike Drugs & High-Alert Drugs
Look-alike, sound-alike (LASA) drugs
Drugs with similar names or appearances that are frequently confused. Published lists by the Institute for Safe Medication Practices (ISMP) and individual institutions. Examples: heparin/hespan, epinephrine/ephedrine, bupivacaine/ropivacaine. Tall-man lettering used to distinguish (e.g., bupivaCaine vs. ropivaCaine).
High-alert drugs
Drugs implicated in an extraordinarily high number of errors with significant patient harm potential. Require additional safeguards, double-checks, and special labeling. Examples: concentrated electrolytes (KCl), anticoagulants, neuromuscular blocking agents, insulin, chemotherapy.
High-alert drugs on the sterile field include: neuromuscular blocking agents (paralytics), concentrated local anesthetics, heparin, thrombin, and epinephrine. These require double-verification before use and careful labeling.
Drug Delivery Devices
Parts of a syringe (the ST must know all):
Barrel — cylindrical body that holds the drug; has measurement markings (mL/cc)
Plunger — moves within the barrel to draw up or expel drug
Tip — the end of the syringe where the needle attaches; Luer-Lok (twist-lock) or slip tip
Hub — the wide base of the needle where it connects to the syringe tip
Shaft — the long cylindrical body of the needle
Bevel — the angled, sharpened tip of the needle; bevel up for most injections to reduce trauma
Other delivery devices:
Needles — various gauges (smaller # = larger bore); lengths vary by route and patient size
IV tubing and pumps — delivers continuous or intermittent IV drugs; infusion pumps provide precise rate control
Dispensers — specialized devices for topical drugs, irrigation syringes, bulb syringes
Measurement systems the ST must know: Metric (grams, milligrams, milliliters — standard in healthcare) · Apothecary (grains, minims — older; rarely used now) · International units (IU) — used for heparin, insulin, penicillin · Roman numerals — may appear on drug labels · Military time (0000–2359) — used in documentation.
Drug Administration Routes
Parenteral routes (bypasses GI tract):
Intravenous (IV)
Fastest onset; into bloodstream directly
Most common perioperative route; immediate effect; most anesthesia and emergency drugs given IV
Intramuscular (IM)
Into muscle tissue
Slower onset than IV; pre-op sedation, some antibiotics, vaccines; gluteal, deltoid, vastus lateralis
Subcutaneous (SQ/SC)
Under skin into fatty tissue
Slow, sustained absorption; insulin, heparin, some hormones; 45–90° angle with short needle
Intradermal (ID)
Into dermis (skin)
Very slow absorption; allergy testing, TB skin test (Mantoux); 10–15° angle; small wheal forms
Intraspinal
Into spinal canal
Intrathecal (into CSF) or epidural (outside dura); used for spinal and epidural anesthesia
Intraosseous (IO)
Into bone marrow
Emergency access when IV unavailable (infants, cardiac arrest); tibia or sternum; same effect as IV
Intraperitoneal
Into peritoneal cavity
Chemotherapy (HIPEC), irrigation; direct drug delivery to abdominal cavity; used in specific oncologic procedures
Oral routes:
Ingestion — swallowed; absorbed via GI tract; slowest onset; pre-op medications
Buccal — placed between cheek and gum; absorbed through mucous membrane
Sublingual (SL) — under tongue; rapid absorption via sublingual veins; nitroglycerin
Topical routes (on body surfaces):
Instillation (drops) Transdermal patch Rectal Vaginal Nasal Inhalant
Pharmacokinetics — ADME
Pharmacokinetics = study of the movement of drugs through the body and the changes that occur IN the drug. Remember with ADME.
Phase 1
Absorption
Movement of drug from site of administration into the bloodstream. Rate depends on route (IV = immediate; oral = slowest), drug formulation, blood flow to the site, and patient factors (GI motility, pH).
Phase 2
Distribution
Movement of drug from blood into tissues and target organs. Affected by: plasma protein binding (bound drug is inactive), lipid solubility (lipid-soluble drugs cross blood-brain barrier), cardiac output, and body composition.
Phase 3
Biotransformation (Metabolism)
Chemical alteration of the drug — primarily in the liver (hepatic metabolism). Converts drugs into metabolites (usually less active). Cytochrome P450 enzyme system. Liver disease significantly affects drug metabolism and dosing.
Phase 4
Excretion (Elimination)
Removal of drug/metabolites from the body — primarily via kidneys (urine). Also: bile (feces), lungs (exhaled anesthetic gases), sweat, saliva. Renal impairment = reduced drug clearance = dose adjustment required.
Pharmacodynamics
Pharmacodynamics = the changes in the body caused by a drug (drug's effect ON the body). Contrast with pharmacokinetics = what the body does TO the drug.
Onset
Time from drug administration until therapeutic effect is first observed; varies by route and drug — IV fastest, oral slowest
Peak effect
Maximum therapeutic effect; time when drug concentration in blood is highest and effect is greatest; varies by drug and route
Agonist
A drug that binds to a receptor and activates it, producing a pharmacological response. Mimics the body's natural signal. Example: morphine is an opioid receptor agonist.
Antagonist
A drug that binds to a receptor and BLOCKS it, preventing activation and reversing the effect of an agonist. Example: naloxone (Narcan) is an opioid receptor antagonist — reverses opioid overdose.
Therapeutic window
The range between the minimum effective dose and the toxic dose. Drugs with narrow therapeutic windows (e.g., digoxin, warfarin, aminoglycosides) require close monitoring. Above = toxicity; below = no effect.
Adverse reactions: Unintended and harmful effects. Drug allergy = immune-mediated response (IgE-mediated anaphylaxis most dangerous). Allergic reaction = any hypersensitivity response. ALWAYS check allergy status before handing any drug to the surgeon — report known allergies to the team.
Surgical Drug Categories
Local anesthetics
Block sodium channels to prevent nerve conduction; lidocaine, bupivacaine, ropivacaine; epinephrine added for vasoconstriction
Blood and blood derivatives
pRBCs, FFP, platelets, cryoprecipitate, albumin; used for hemorrhage, coagulopathy, hypovolemia
Hemostatic agents
Promote clotting on the sterile field: thrombin, Gelfoam, Surgicel, bone wax, epinephrine-soaked pledgets
Anticoagulants & thrombolytics
Heparin (intraop), warfarin, enoxaparin; tPA for clot dissolution; used in vascular, cardiac cases
CNS agents
Drugs affecting brain and spinal cord; includes anesthetic induction agents (propofol, etomidate, ketamine)
Neuromuscular blocking agents
Paralytics: succinylcholine (depolarizing), rocuronium, vecuronium (non-depolarizing); cause complete muscle relaxation; HIGH-ALERT drug
Analgesics
Pain management: opioids (morphine, fentanyl, hydromorphone), NSAIDs, acetaminophen; used pre-, intra-, and post-op
Sedatives and hypnotics
Benzodiazepines (midazolam), propofol; used for sedation, amnesia, and induction of anesthesia
Contrast media
Iodine-based contrast for angiography, cholangiography; gadolinium for MRI; allergy risk; sterile on field for intraop imaging
Colored dyes and stains
Methylene blue (ureteral identification), indigo carmine, isosulfan blue (sentinel node mapping); kept sterile on field
Antiinfective agents
Prophylactic antibiotics (cefazolin most common pre-op); topical antibiotics on field; given within 60 min before incision
Antineoplastic agents
Chemotherapy; HIPEC (intraperitoneal); extreme hazard — require special handling protocols, PPE
Autonomic nervous system drugs
Epinephrine (adrenergic), atropine (anticholinergic), neostigmine; affect heart rate, BP, bronchodilation
Fluid balance & electrolytes
Normal saline, LR, D5W; potassium chloride (HIGH-ALERT); sodium bicarbonate; maintain fluid status and electrolytes
Cardiac drugs
Lidocaine (antiarrhythmic), adenosine, amiodarone, atropine; used during cardiac surgery and code situations
Diuretics
Furosemide (Lasix), mannitol; promote urine output; mannitol used in neurosurgery to reduce ICP
GI drugs
Metoclopramide (aspiration prophylaxis), famotidine (antacid), ondansetron (anti-nausea)
Hormones & synthetic substitutes
Oxytocin (uterine contraction), vasopressin, DDAVP; specific to procedure and patient needs
Corticosteroids
Dexamethasone, methylprednisolone; anti-inflammatory; used for cerebral edema, joint injections, PONV prophylaxis
Antidiabetic drugs
Insulin (HIGH-ALERT); glucose; perioperative glucose management critical for wound healing and infection risk
Emergency drugs
Epinephrine (anaphylaxis, cardiac arrest), atropine, vasopressors; immediately accessible in every OR; ST must recognize emergency situations
Drug Error Prevention — 10 Influencing Elements
A drug error is a mistake made at any stage of the medication process. Drug errors can result in severe injury or death. The ST is responsible for preventing errors within their scope of practice.
1. Patient information
Accurate patient identification, allergy status, weight, and medical history must be available and verified before any medication is given
2. Lack of knowledge about the drug
Insufficient understanding of drug actions, dosages, interactions, or contraindications; education and reference resource use is the remedy
3. Communication among team members
Verbal orders, handoffs, and unclear instructions; closed-loop communication and read-back protocols are essential
4. Drug packaging, labeling, and nomenclature
Look-alike/sound-alike packaging; similar vial sizes and colors; errors from reading labels hastily; LASA drugs require extra verification
5. Drug stock, storage, standardization, and distribution
Poor inventory control, non-standardized concentrations, incorrect storage temperature, or missing drugs when urgently needed
6. Environmental factors
OR noise, distractions, poor lighting, high workload, fatigue, and time pressure all contribute to medication errors
7. Drug device acquisition, use, and monitoring
Infusion pump programming errors; incorrect tubing; failure to monitor drug delivery; device malfunction
8. Staff competency and education
Undertrained or unfamiliar staff; new drug introduction without proper education; competency assessments required for drug handling
9. Patient education on medications
Patient unaware of their own medications, allergies, or herbal supplements; pre-op assessment and medication reconciliation prevent errors
10. Quality processes and risk management
Lack of incident reporting, no error analysis, no safety culture; institutions must have systems to detect, report, analyze, and prevent errors
Organizing Drugs on the Sterile Field
Mixing and preparing drugs
Some drugs require dilution (e.g., epinephrine for local field use) or mixing before use. Prepare according to the pharmacy/manufacturer instructions. Double-check concentration after preparation and label immediately.
Preventing air introduction
When drawing up drugs: point needle upward to allow bubbles to rise, tap syringe, expel air before use. Air embolism from air-filled syringes is a serious hazard. Never inject air into a patient.
Dispensing to the surgeon
Always state the concentration and amount when handing a loaded syringe to the surgeon: "This is 1% lidocaine with epinephrine, 10 mL." Surgeon must verbally acknowledge before accepting.
Organization strategy
Keep drugs separated on the field; organize by type or use; never mix unlabeled medications; keep irrigation solutions separate from injectable drugs to prevent inadvertent injection of irrigation fluid
Required Documentation After Drug Administration
Drug name Dose Amount Route Time Name of person administering Patient assessment / response
Documentation is required for every drug administered — including drugs used on the sterile field. The intraoperative record is a legal document. All information must be complete, accurate, and recorded in real time.
